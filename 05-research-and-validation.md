# Story, animation and game-design research / validation agenda

This is a planning synthesis, **not** a claim that a primary-source research review has already been completed.

## Storytelling and character craft to study
- Emma Coats's widely circulated Pixar story guidelines: characters with convictions and desires; emotional causal chains; characters admired for effort rather than only success. Treat them as a practitioner's heuristics, not official studio doctrine.
- The classic twelve principles of animation associated with Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston: staging, anticipation, squash-and-stretch, arcs, timing, appeal, exaggeration and follow-through. Apply selectively to game animation.
- Three-beat comedy: setup, expectation, visual surprise. Make first ride reactions worth watching repeatedly.
- Character triangles: Pip's optimism vs cautious rider's anxiety vs thrill-seeker's impatience, with comedian as wildcard.
- Agency and authorship: mission should motivate creativity without reducing play to executing Pip's instructions.

## Games and UX comparisons worth examining
- *Astro Bot*: goal clarity, environmental humor, character attachment and discovery.
- Child-friendly creation tools: constraints that prevent blank-canvas paralysis, undoability, tactile affordances.
- Theme park / coaster games: legible tracks, simplified building, satisfying first result, expressive camera modes.

## Testable hypotheses
1. A short story introduction increases children's motivation to build compared with an immediate editor.
2. Characters with distinct personalities make reactions feel like real rewards.
3. A guaranteed Spark paired with occasional surprise rare variants encourages replay without making the child feel evaluated.
4. Cosmetics that animate within the next experience are more satisfying than static inventory icons.
5. A short guided mission creates an effective bridge to more open-ended construction.

## Playtest structure
Recruit a small mixed set of age-appropriate participants with parental participation/permission. Observe without coaching. Test story comprehension, emotional attachment, creation/ride success, reaction to collection, and desire to replay. Capture quotes and moments of genuine surprise; avoid over-relying on “Did you like it?” questions.

## Decision gates
- Do kids know **what** they're trying to accomplish and **why**?
- Do they laugh or express anticipation during the character ride?
- Is there an unprompted request for another ride?
- Does the cosmetic make them care about the characters more?
- Can they invent something of their own after the guided first mission?

## Research still to do
